## Abstract

Maps of philosophical positions are built from pairwise relations such as distances, correlations and shared citations. A synthesis of commitments can be compatible in every pair and incompatible as a whole. We ask when checking $k$ commitments at a time decides joint compatibility, and how much structure in position data lies beyond pairwise statistics. The first answer is the obstruction order of the commitment class, the size of its largest minimal incompatible family, which is its Helly number. Categorical answers and ranges on ordered scales have order two, so pairwise maps of answer profiles are complete. On $n$ yes/no issues, implication rules and parity constraints have order $n+1$ and all two-literal clauses $2n$; convex regions have order $d+1$ in a continuous space, $2^d$ on a grid of ordinal positions, and at least six when evaluated on 30 occupied positions of a planar map. Categorical answers and conditional principles, of orders two and one, combine to order $n+1$. Exhaustive enumeration confirms the small cases. Pairwise statistics of observed answers determine exactly their majority-closed hull, so parity-type exclusions are invisible to them. Individual PhilPapers survey responses are not public, so two public datasets were tested. In the judgments of 64,988 participants on three trolley cases, third-order structure carries 2.38% of the multi-information and is stable across countries and designs. In the answers of 294 respondents to a survey on consciousness science none is detectable, at a sample size with power 0.29 for an effect of the trolley size.

## Pairwise maps and joint commitments

Three positions about the trolley cases show the problem this paper studies. Let $A$ be the view that pushing a man off a footbridge to stop a runaway trolley and save five people is permissible, $B$ the view that diverting the trolley onto a side track, where it kills one person instead of five, is impermissible, and $C$ the principle that if the pushing is permissible, so is the diverting. Any two of the three can be held together. All three cannot. A map that records how $A$, $B$ and $C$ relate in pairs, whether by distances, correlations or a graph of compatible pairs, shows three mutually compatible positions and has no place to register that the three exclude each other.

Maps that place philosophical positions or works relative to one another are mostly built from pairwise relations of this kind. The PhilPapers surveys report correlations between answers and factor analyses of them [@bourget2014; @bourget2023]. A bibliometric map of recent philosophy compares articles by the references they share and projects the resulting similarities onto a plane [@noichl2021]. The correlation structure of philosophers' answers now also serves as a benchmark for language models that imitate philosophers [@shi2026]. Topic models of philosophy journals map subjects instead of positions, tracing what philosophers wrote about over a century [@malaterre2019; @weatherson2022]. Maps of positions are read for what goes with what, and also for which combinations are unoccupied and which syntheses might be attempted. The last two readings concern joint compatibility, which pairwise data do not record in general.

This paper asks when they do. Given a class of commitments, when does the compatibility of every $k$ members of a family decide whether the whole family can be held at once, and how large must $k$ be for the commitment classes that maps of positions use? The second question is empirical: in public data on positions about philosophical questions, how much structure lies beyond what pairwise statistics capture?

The answer to the first question is a number attached to the representation class: the largest size of a minimal incompatible family, which convex geometry calls the Helly number of the class [@danzer1963]. A map that checks $k$ commitments at a time decides joint compatibility for every family from the class exactly when this number is at most $k$. Profiles of categorical answers and of ranges on ordered scales have order two, so pairwise maps of them are complete. Order grows once commitments carry inferential links or geometric shape. On $n$ yes/no issues, implication rules and parity constraints reach $n+1$ and arbitrary two-literal clauses $2n$. Convex regions reach $d+1$ in a continuous $d$-dimensional space, $2^d$ on a grid of ordinal positions, and at least six when they are evaluated on thirty occupied positions of a planar map in general position. Two classes of low order, categorical answers and conditional principles, combine to order $n+1$. The number of commitments a check must inspect is a different quantity from the number of issues it must inspect: categorical answers joined by implications have unbounded commitment order, yet propagation over two issues at a time refutes every incompatible family of them. Pairwise statistics of observed positions determine exactly the majority-closed hull of the observed answer patterns, so a parity-type exclusion is invisible to them.

The empirical part uses two public datasets, because individual responses to the PhilPapers surveys are not public. In the judgments of 64,988 participants on three trolley cases [@awad2020], third-order structure is present, stable across countries and design conditions, and small: 2.38% of the multi-information. In the answers of 294 respondents to a survey on the foundations of consciousness science [@francken2022], third-order terms do not improve held-out prediction and no triplet of answers survives correction for multiple testing, at a sample size that detects an effect of the trolley size with probability 0.29.

The local-to-global facts used here come from several fields: Helly-type theorems in convex geometry, near-unanimity operations and local consistency in constraint satisfaction, acyclic schemes in databases, the median property and quota rules in judgment aggregation, minimal unsatisfiable sets in logic and diagnosis, contextuality, and connected information in statistical physics. Several of the orders below are classical and are credited to their sources, as is the Helly-type reading of near-unanimity operations, which is due to Feder and Vardi. What this paper adds is the statement of these facts as one condition on maps of positions, short proofs of the orders of the commitment classes such maps use, the occupancy reading of planar maps, the characterization of what pairwise statistics of observed positions can certify, exhaustive verification of the small cases in a recorded run, and the measurement of higher-order structure in public data.

## Commitments, obstructions and the order of a class

Let $\Omega$ be a set of complete positions, for example all combinations of answers to a fixed list of questions, or the points of a conceptual space. A commitment is a subset $C\subseteq\Omega$, the positions that satisfy it. A finite family of commitments is compatible when some position satisfies all of them, that is, when their intersection is nonempty. An obstruction is an incompatible family whose proper subfamilies are all compatible. Under other names, obstructions are minimal unsatisfiable subsets of constraints [@liffiton2008], minimal conflict sets in diagnosis [@reiter1987], kernels of a contradiction in belief contraction [@hansson1994], minimally inconsistent subsets of an agenda in judgment aggregation [@dietrich2007], and aporetic clusters of individually plausible theses that cannot all be held [@rescher2009]. In each of these literatures a repair must remove at least one member of every obstruction: diagnoses are minimal hitting sets of the conflict sets [@reiter1987], and minimal correction sets are minimal hitting sets of the minimal unsatisfiable subsets [@liffiton2008].

A representation class $\mathcal C$ is the set of commitments an atlas can express. Its obstruction order $\omega(\mathcal C)$, or order for short, is the largest number of members of an obstruction drawn from $\mathcal C$, with $\omega(\mathcal C)=1$ if every finite family from $\mathcal C$ is compatible and $\omega(\mathcal C)=\infty$ if obstructions of every size exist. This is the Helly number of $\mathcal C$: the least $k$ such that a finite family from $\mathcal C$ is compatible whenever each $k$ of its members are [@danzer1963; @averkov2013].

**Proposition 1** (what a $k$-wise map decides). Every finite family from $\mathcal C$ whose subfamilies of at most $k$ members are compatible is itself compatible if and only if $\omega(\mathcal C)\le k$.

*Proof.* Deleting members of an incompatible finite family for as long as it stays incompatible ends at an obstruction. If $\omega(\mathcal C)\le k$, that obstruction has at most $k$ members, so the family fails the $k$-wise test. An obstruction with more than $k$ members passes the $k$-wise test and is incompatible. $\square$

For a finite $\Omega$, the Jaccard distance $d_J(A,B)=1-|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$ between two nonempty commitments is smaller than one exactly when they are compatible. A distance map of commitments therefore records pairwise compatibility, and by Proposition 1 it decides joint compatibility for every family from $\mathcal C$ exactly when $\omega(\mathcal C)\le 2$. Above order two, finer pairwise information does not close the gap in general. In a standard construction on $m$ yes/no issues, the commitments $x_1=0,\dots,x_m=0$ together with $x_1+\dots+x_m\equiv b\pmod 2$ form a compatible family for $b=0$ and an obstruction for $b=1$, while every pair of commitments has Jaccard distance $2/3$ in both families and every proper subfamily has the same number of satisfying positions in both. The recorded run checks this for families of 3 to 12 commitments, 8,164 proper subfamilies in all.

Two further facts make the order a property of the representation, and a computable one.

**Proposition 2** (bundling commitments into positions). Let $\mathcal C^{\cap}$ be the class of finite intersections of members of $\mathcal C$, which contains every profile an atlas can describe by conjoining commitments. Then $\omega(\mathcal C^{\cap})=\omega(\mathcal C)$.

*Proof.* The inequality $\omega(\mathcal C^{\cap})\ge\omega(\mathcal C)$ is immediate. Let $B_1,\dots,B_s$ be an obstruction in $\mathcal C^{\cap}$, with $B_i=\bigcap K_i$ for finite $K_i\subseteq\mathcal C$, and choose $p_i\in\bigcap_{j\ne i}B_j$. The family $\bigcup_i K_i$ is incompatible and contains an obstruction $M$ of $\mathcal C$. Some member of $M$ excludes $p_i$; since $p_i$ lies in every $B_j$ with $j\ne i$, that member belongs to $K_i$ and to no other $K_j$. Distinct indices have distinct such members, so $s\le|M|\le\omega(\mathcal C)$. $\square$

For Helly numbers the identity is standard [@averkov2013, eq. 3.1]. For an atlas it means that bundling atomic commitments into the profiles of schools, authors or works never changes the order: the order is fixed by the atomic commitment types the atlas admits.

**Proposition 3** (reading the order from positions). Let $\mathcal C$ be closed under finite intersections and let $\mathrm{cl}(A)$ be the smallest member of $\mathcal C$ containing $A$, or $\Omega$ if there is none. Then $\omega(\mathcal C)$ is the largest size of a finite set $F\subseteq\Omega$ with $\bigcap_{p\in F}\mathrm{cl}(F\setminus\{p\})=\varnothing$.

*Proof.* For such an $F$ the sets $\mathrm{cl}(F\setminus\{p\})$ form an obstruction, because the subfamily that omits $\mathrm{cl}(F\setminus\{p\})$ contains $p$. Conversely, given an obstruction $B_1,\dots,B_s$ and points $p_i\in\bigcap_{j\ne i}B_j$, the points are distinct and each $\mathrm{cl}(\{p_j:j\ne i\})$ lies in $B_i$, so the intersection of these closures is empty. $\square$

For convex sets restricted to a set of points this is Hoffman's characterization [@hoffman1979; @amenta2017, Lemma 2.7]. If $F$ has the property, so does $G=F\setminus\{q\}$, since $\bigcap_{p\in G}\mathrm{cl}(G\setminus\{p\})$ is contained in $\mathrm{cl}(F\setminus\{q\})\cap\bigcap_{p\in G}\mathrm{cl}(F\setminus\{p\})$. The largest such $F$ can therefore be found by extending sets of positions one at a time and discarding every set that fails. All orders reported below as computed were found this way, and checked against a direct enumeration of subfamilies where that was feasible.

### Answer profiles

Suppose every commitment constrains a single question $q$: it says that the answer lies in a set of admissible answers drawn from a class $\mathcal S_q$ of subsets of the answer set $D_q$. A family of such commitments is incompatible exactly when, for some single question, the answer sets it imposes have empty intersection. An obstruction therefore lies on one question, and the order of the class is the largest of the orders $\omega(\mathcal S_q)$. By Proposition 2 the same holds for conjunctions of such commitments, that is, for partial answer profiles. Call this the product lemma.

Three cases cover most survey-based atlases. A categorical answer, such as "accepts physicalism", excludes every other answer to the same question, two different answers already conflict, and profiles of categorical answers have order two. A range on an ordinal scale, such as "agrees at least to degree four of five", is an interval of a line; intervals have order two, and so do their products, the axis-parallel boxes [@danzer1963, p. 144; @amenta2017, Theorem 2.2]. An exclusion on a question with $k$ exhaustive options has order $k$: if the options of a normative-ethics question are taken to be consequentialism, deontology and virtue ethics, the commitments "not consequentialism", "not deontology" and "not virtue ethics" are compatible in pairs and jointly incompatible. An atlas of answer profiles without exclusions is thus complete at the pairwise level. Two partial profiles are compatible exactly when they give different answers to no common question, and a family of profiles is compatible exactly when its members are pairwise compatible.

### Inferential links between yes/no issues

Philosophical commitments are often conditional or relational: "if physicalism is true, zombies are impossible", "not both libertarian free will and determinism". On $n$ yes/no issues every commitment is a set of answer profiles, and the classes in Table 1 are generated by clauses of fixed shapes. Horn rules, two-literal clauses and parity constraints are preserved, respectively, by the conjunction, the majority and the exclusive or of three answer profiles, three of the operations that make a Boolean constraint language tractable in Schaefer's classification [@schaefer1978; @chen2006]. By the argument of Proposition 2, an obstruction of $s$ commitments from such a class contains, through the members private to each commitment, a minimal unsatisfiable set of at least $s$ clauses of the generating shape. Bounds on minimal unsatisfiable clause sets therefore bound the order.

Implications alone never conflict, because the all-yes profile and the all-no profile satisfy every implication $x_i\to x_j$; the order is one. Categorical answers together with implications have order $n+1$. The chain $x_1,\ x_1\to x_2,\ \dots,\ x_{n-1}\to x_n,\ \neg x_n$ is an obstruction of $n+1$ commitments: deleting $x_1$ allows the all-no profile, deleting $\neg x_n$ the all-yes profile, and deleting a link $x_i\to x_{i+1}$ the profile that answers yes up to $x_i$ and no afterwards. The upper bound holds for the larger class of Horn rules, whose clauses contain at most one positive literal ("if $A$ and $B$ then $C$", "not both $A$ and $B$"). Forward chaining from the facts of an unsatisfiable Horn set must derive every condition of some clause without a positive literal; otherwise the profile that answers yes exactly on the derived issues satisfies the set. The clause that first derives each issue, together with that negative clause, already forms an unsatisfiable set. A minimal unsatisfiable Horn set therefore has at most one clause per issue and one negative clause, $n+1$ in all.

Categorical answers with exclusions $\neg(x_i\wedge x_j)$ have order three. They are Horn rules whose only derivations are the facts themselves, so a minimal unsatisfiable set consists of one negative clause and the facts it contradicts: $\{x_i,\neg x_i\}$ or $\{x_i,x_j,\neg(x_i\wedge x_j)\}$.

Parity constraints $x_{i_1}+\dots+x_{i_r}\equiv b\pmod 2$ describe affine subspaces of $\{0,1\}^n$ over the field with two elements and have order $n+1$. If $A_1,\dots,A_s$ is an obstruction and $p_i\in\bigcap_{j\ne i}A_j$, the points $p_i$ are affinely independent: were $p_i$ an affine combination of the other points, all of which lie in the affine subspace $A_i$, it would lie in $A_i$ and hence in every member. At most $n+1$ points of $\{0,1\}^n$ are affinely independent. The parity family of the previous section, with $m=n$, attains the bound.

All two-literal clauses, which include categorical answers and the commitments "$A$ or $B$", "not both $A$ and $B$" and "if $A$ then $B$", have order $2n$. A clause $a\vee b$ licenses the implications $\neg a\to b$ and $\neg b\to a$, and a set of such clauses is unsatisfiable exactly when, for some issue $x$, its implication graph contains a path from $x$ to $\neg x$ and a path from $\neg x$ to $x$ [@aspvall1979]. Follow a path from $x$ to $\neg x$ only until it first reaches the complement $\neg y$ of a literal $y$ it has already visited. The literals before that point concern distinct issues, so this segment uses at most $n$ clauses, and continuing from $\neg y$ along the contrapositive of the segment from $x$ to $y$ reaches $\neg x$ through clauses already used. The same holds from $\neg x$ to $x$, so every unsatisfiable set of two-literal clauses has an unsatisfiable subset of at most $2n$ clauses. The $2n$ clauses of the two cycles $x_1\to x_2\to\dots\to x_n\to\neg x_1$ and $\neg x_1\to\neg x_2\to\dots\to\neg x_n\to x_1$ attain the bound. The first cycle forces $\neg x_1$ and the second forces $x_1$. Deleting a link $x_i\to x_{i+1}$ of the first cycle allows the profile that answers yes up to $x_i$ and no afterwards, deleting $x_n\to\neg x_1$ allows the all-yes profile, and the second cycle is the image of the first under exchanging yes and no. Both the bound and the extremal family are known in the study of minimal unsatisfiability. Lee used the same implication paths to bound such sets by $4n-2$ clauses [@lee2009]. In the classification of Abbasizanjani and Kullmann, the two cycles are the cycle of equivalences $x_1\leftrightarrow x_2\leftrightarrow\dots\leftrightarrow x_n\leftrightarrow\neg x_1$, to which elimination of singular variables reduces every minimally unsatisfiable set of two-literal clauses with at least two more clauses than variables [@abbasizanjani2020]; since that elimination preserves the difference between clauses and variables, the bound of $2n$ follows. The truncation argument above gives it directly. Clauses with more literals grow faster: Lee constructed minimally unsatisfiable sets of $k$-literal clauses with a number of clauses of order $n^k$ for every $k\ge3$ [@lee2009], and at most $2^k\binom nk$ such clauses exist, so the order of $k$-literal clauses grows like $n^k$.

Unrestricted commitments have order $2^n$, attained by the $2^n$ commitments that each exclude one profile; no class over a finite $\Omega$ exceeds $|\Omega|$.

The order of a union of classes can exceed the orders of its parts. Categorical answers have order two and implications order one, but together they have order $n+1$. In a map of positions this is the common case: categorical answers are linked by conditional principles, each harmless alone, and a chain of $n-1$ conditionals linking $n$ questions produces an obstruction of $n+1$ commitments that no check of fewer commitments detects. The trolley triad of the opening is the chain with $n=2$.

### Regions of a conceptual space

Gärdenfors proposed that natural properties correspond to convex regions of the quality dimensions of a conceptual space [@gardenfors2000]. If positions are points of $\mathbb R^d$ and commitments are convex regions, the order is $d+1$ by Helly's theorem [@helly1923; @danzer1963]. Axis-parallel boxes, the ranges of the previous subsection, have order two in every dimension.

Survey positions are not points of a continuum. If each of $d$ quality dimensions is measured on an ordinal scale with at least two levels, the positions form a grid, and a convex commitment admits the grid positions inside a convex region, as linear threshold commitments do ("the mean agreement with items one and two is at least three"). Convex sets restricted to the integer lattice have order $2^d$ [@doignon1973; @averkov2013], and the bound is attained on any such grid: each of the $2^d$ commitments that admits the vertices of one unit cell except one vertex is cut out by a convex region, and together they form an obstruction. The same convex commitments thus need checks of $d+1$ at a time in the continuum and of $2^d$ at a time on a grid of Likert positions.

A map can also be read for occupancy. Suppose it records the positions actually held, by people or by works, as a finite set $S$, and counts a synthesis as available when some recorded position satisfies every commitment. The commitments are then convex regions evaluated on $S$, and their order equals the largest number of positions of $S$ in convex position whose convex hull contains no other position of $S$ [@hoffman1979; @averkov2013; see @amenta2017, Lemma 2.6]. One direction is immediate: if $G\subseteq S$ is such a set, the regions $\mathrm{conv}(G\setminus\{g\})$ evaluated on $S$ admit exactly $G\setminus\{g\}$, and the $|G|$ of them form an obstruction. In the plane, every 10 positions in general position contain such a set of five, an empty convex pentagon [@harborth1978], and every 30 contain an empty convex hexagon [@heule2024]. Any planar map of 30 occupied positions in general position therefore has order at least six for convex commitments. Arbitrarily large sets without an empty convex heptagon exist [@horton1983], positions in convex position give order $m$, and for $m$ uniformly random positions the largest empty convex polygon grows like $\log m/\log\log m$ [@balogh2013].

The difference between the plane and its occupied points matters when a map is read for gaps. Helly's bound of three concerns whether regions share a point of the plane, a possible position. Whether they share an occupied position is a separate question of larger order. A family of regions can share points of the plane and no occupied position, which marks a combination that is possible and unoccupied; on a planar map of 30 positions in general position, no check of fewer than six regions at a time can certify all such combinations.

### Exhaustive verification

The recorded run computes the order of each class by the search of Proposition 3 and compares it with the formulas. For yes/no issues it covers seven classes for $n=2$ to $5$ and unrestricted commitments for $n=2$ to $4$: all 31 computed orders equal the formulas of Table 1, and for $n\le 3$ an independent enumeration of all subfamilies of atomic commitments agrees in all 24 cases. Replacing the atoms by all their conjunctions leaves the order unchanged for each of the 8 classes at $n=2$. The lower-bound families (chains, parity families, double cycles, exclusion triples) are confirmed as obstructions for $n$ up to 10, and the unit-cell families on grids with three levels for $d$ up to 4. Single-question classes, their products and $L\times L$ grids give the stated orders in all 32 cases. ## Locality in commitments and locality in issues

### A background logic of admissible positions

Many atlases fix which complete profiles are admissible, the set $X\subseteq\Omega$ of profiles consistent with accepted principles, and record only categorical answers. A family of categorical answers is compatible within $X$ when some admissible profile gives all of them. Universal algebra supplies a sufficient condition for low order. A $(k+1)$-ary near-unanimity operation returns $a$ whenever all but at most one of its arguments equal $a$; majority is the ternary case. Baker and Pixley proved that a relation closed under such an operation is determined by its projections onto sets of $k$ coordinates [@baker1975].

**Proposition 4.** If $X$ is closed under a $(k+1)$-ary near-unanimity operation applied issue by issue, every family of categorical answers that is $k$-wise compatible within $X$ is compatible within $X$. For yes/no issues and $k=2$ the converse holds: if $X$ is not closed under majority, some obstruction consists of at least three categorical answers.

*Proof.* A family of categorical answers is a partial profile $t$ on a set $J$ of issues, compatible within $X$ when $t$ belongs to the projection of $X$ onto $J$. Projections of $X$ are closed under the same operation, and $k$-wise compatibility says that every restriction of $t$ to at most $k$ issues lies in the corresponding projection, so $t$ lies in the projection by the Baker-Pixley theorem. For the converse, the profiles each pair of whose answers occurs together in some admissible profile form a set defined by two-literal clauses, which is closed under majority: of three profiles that satisfy a two-literal clause, two satisfy the same one of its literals, and so does their majority. If $X$ is not closed under majority, this set strictly contains $X$, and a profile in the difference supplies a family of categorical answers that is pairwise compatible within $X$ and incompatible. Its obstructions have at least three members. $\square$

Feder and Vardi studied the same property for every instance of a constraint language at once. They call it the $l$-Helly property: whenever fixing the values of some variables leaves an instance without a solution, some $l$ of those values already do. They proved it equivalent to an $(l+1)$-ary near-unanimity operation preserving the constraints and to strict width $l$, the property that local inference followed by greedy assignment always finds a solution [@feder1998]. Proposition 4 is the version for one background set of admissible profiles, which is the situation of an atlas. What counts as background changes the order. With the implications $x_1\to x_2,\dots,x_{n-1}\to x_n$ as background, the answers $x_1$ and $\neg x_n$ already conflict, an obstruction of two categorical answers; when the implications are themselves commitments under examination, every obstruction contains them, and the chain has $n+1$ members.

Judgment aggregation measures the same quantity. Dietrich and List showed that a uniform quota rule, which accepts a proposition when at least $m$ of $n$ individuals accept it, turns every profile of consistent individual judgments into consistent collective judgments exactly when $m>n-n/z$, where $z$ is the size of the largest minimally inconsistent subset of the agenda [@dietrich2007, Corollary 2]. The number $z$ is the order of the agenda's categorical answers in the sense used here. For an odd number of individuals, issue-by-issue majority voting is therefore consistent if and only if $z\le2$, the property that characterizes the median spaces of Nehring and Puppe [@nehring2007] and the case $k=2$ of Proposition 4. For preferences over $q$ options the largest minimally inconsistent sets are cycles of $q$ pairwise preferences, so $z=q$ and the classical supermajority thresholds for acyclic collective preferences follow [@dietrich2007, Section 3.3]. Greenberg's condition for majority equilibria in spatial voting, a quota above $dn/(d+1)$ for convex preferences over a $d$-dimensional compact convex set of alternatives [@greenberg1979], has the same form with $z=d+1$, the order of convex commitments in $d$ dimensions. The discursive dilemma of List and Pettit is the smallest case [@list2002]: with $X$ the profiles that satisfy $r\leftrightarrow(p\wedge q)$, the majority of the admissible profiles $(1,1,1)$, $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,1,0)$ is $(1,1,0)$, which is not admissible, and $\{p,q,\neg r\}$ is an obstruction of three categorical answers.

The recorded run checks the case $k=2$ of Proposition 4 on every nonempty set of admissible profiles over three issues, 255 sets of which 165 are closed under majority, and on 3,000 random sets over four issues. In every case the order of categorical answers is at most two exactly when the set is closed under majority.

### Checks that inspect a few issues at a time

A second kind of locality concerns issues. Constraint satisfaction decides many problems by local consistency: partial profiles on a few issues that satisfy the constraints among them are extended one issue at a time, and inconsistency shows when some issue has no admissible answer left. Jeavons, Cohen and Cooper characterized algebraically the constraint types for which strong $k$-consistency is enough to ensure global consistency [@jeavons1998]. Feder and Vardi characterized the problems solved by such local methods through Datalog and showed that systems of linear equations over Abelian groups, which include parity constraints, are not among them [@feder1998]; Barto and Kozik proved that every problem without this ability to count is solved by local consistency [@barto2014]. In database theory, a scheme of relations is acyclic if and only if every database over it whose relations are pairwise consistent is globally consistent [@beeri1983], and Vorob'ev characterized by a similar condition the families of marginal distributions that always extend to a joint distribution [@vorobev1962]. Contextuality is the failure of such an extension: families of local data that agree wherever they overlap and admit no global assignment [@abramsky2011].

These results bound the number of issues a check inspects. The number of commitments it must inspect is a different quantity. The chain of the previous section is an obstruction of $n+1$ commitments, so no check of fewer commitments detects it, yet propagation over pairs of issues refutes it: the fact $x_1$ removes the answer no from $x_2$, and so on, until the commitment $\neg x_n$ leaves $x_n$ without an answer. The scopes of its commitments form a path, which is acyclic, and acyclicity does not bound its order. The recorded run confirms the propagation for chains on up to 10 issues. Parity constraints are non-local in both senses, and categorical answers are local in both. A pairwise map of questions, such as a correlation network, and a pairwise map of commitments, such as a distance matrix, therefore answer different questions, and an atlas needs to say which kind of locality it relies on.

Abstract argumentation meets the same distinction. Dung's frameworks record conflicts as a binary attack relation, and a set of arguments is conflict-free when it contains no arguments $A$ and $B$ such that $A$ attacks $B$ [@dung1995]. Conflict is pairwise by construction, so an obstruction of three arguments has to be encoded as an attack by a set of arguments, as in the framework of Nielsen and Parsons [@nielsen2007].

Whether the order of a commitment language stays bounded as the number of issues grows is a property of the language. If it does, the minimal incompatible families form finitely many patterns up to renaming of issues, which in constraint satisfaction is finite duality; conversely, under finite duality every minimal incompatible family is the image of one of finitely many patterns and so has bounded size. Finite duality is equivalent to the definability of the satisfiability problem in first-order logic, a result of Atserias, and it can be decided [@larose2007, Theorem 2.5]. Categorical answers with exclusions have bounded order, categorical answers with implications do not.

## What pairwise statistics of observed positions can see

Maps built from data record pairwise statistics: correlations between answers, two-way contingency tables, distances between respondents. The maximum-entropy distribution consistent with the marginals of order $k$ is the least structured distribution that reproduces them, and the connected information of order $k$ is the entropy that the marginals of order $k$ remove beyond those of order $k-1$. The multi-information of a set of answers is the sum of its connected informations [@schneidman2003; @amari2001]. Computed for groups of ten retinal ganglion cells, the pairwise model captured about 90% of the multi-information [@schneidman2006], and Schneidman and colleagues used three variables joined by exclusive or, whose pairwise mutual informations vanish while their multi-information is one bit, as the example of structure that pairwise statistics miss [@schneidman2003]. The same decomposition measures how much of the structure of a set of positions a pairwise map can represent.

Pairwise statistics also have a logical limit, which is the statistical counterpart of Proposition 4.

**Proposition 5** (the pairwise hull). Let $S\subseteq\{0,1\}^n$ be the set of answer profiles observed in a dataset. The profiles each pair of whose answers occurs together in some observed profile form the smallest set that contains $S$ and is closed under majority. Every distribution with the pairwise marginals of the data assigns probability zero outside this set.

*Proof.* The set $H(S)$ of profiles each pair of whose answers occurs in $S$ is defined by two-literal clauses, hence closed under majority as in the proof of Proposition 4, and it contains $S$. A majority-closed set containing $S$ is determined by its projections onto pairs of issues [@baker1975], which contain those of $S$, so it contains $H(S)$. A profile outside $H(S)$ has a pair of answers that never occurs in the data, and any distribution with the data's pairwise marginals gives that pair, and therefore the profile, probability zero. $\square$

A combination absent from the data is therefore certified absent by a pairwise map only when it lies outside the majority-closed hull of the observed profiles. Absences inside the hull are invisible to every pairwise statistic. The exclusive-or example is the simplest case: if the observed profiles of three answers are exactly those with an even number of yes answers, every pair of answers occurs, the hull contains all eight profiles, and no pairwise statistic can show that the four odd profiles never occur. The recorded run checks the hull identity on every nonempty set of profiles over three issues and on 3,000 random sets over four and five issues.

Third-order structure in population data need not reflect anyone's commitments: a population that mixes groups with different pairwise structures can show higher-order structure that no group shows. The tests below control for the mixing of countries and of design conditions; mixing of unobserved groups remains possible.

## Higher-order structure in two public datasets

### Data and methods

Individual responses to the PhilPapers surveys are not publicly released. The 2009 survey published contingency tables for every pair of answers [@bourget2014], and the 2020 survey reports aggregate distributions without individual-level responses [@shi2026]. Two public datasets of individual positions on philosophical questions, both deposited on the Open Science Framework, were used instead.

The first comes from the classic mode of the Moral Machine website, which presented three versions of the trolley case in random order [@awad2020]. In Switch, a switch diverts a runaway boxcar onto a side track where it hits one person instead of five; in Loop, the side track loops back and the one person's body stops the boxcar; in Footbridge, a large person can be pushed from a footbridge to stop it. The case goes back to Foot, and the loop and footbridge variants to Thomson [@foot2002; @thomson1985]. Answers are coded 1 when the participant chose the sacrifice. The analysis keeps one complete session per participant, the first in the file in which each case was answered once: 64,988 participants. The sacrifice was chosen by 83.4% in Switch, 74.6% in Loop and 54.6% in Footbridge, the ordering that Awad and colleagues found in every country.

The second is a survey on theoretical foundations of consciousness science, promoted mainly among attendees of the 2018 and 2019 conferences of the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness [@francken2022]. Its 307 respondents answered 11 questions on positions in the philosophy of mind and the science of consciousness, among them whether a philosophical zombie is possible given the laws of physics, whether Mary learns something new on first seeing red, whether machines could be conscious, and whether anything would remain to be explained once all functional properties of consciousness are explained. Ten of the questions were answered on five-point scales from "definitely yes" to "definitely no"; one, on whether the transition from unconscious to conscious processing is sharp, ran from a sharp boundary to a continuous transition, with three answers in the 2018 waves and five in 2019. Respondents also rated thirteen entities, from a rock and a thermostat to a dog and other people, on whether they are conscious. An answer of 1 or 2 counts as endorsement. The 294 respondents who answered all 11 position questions form the position battery. The entity battery drops the 5 entities on which fewer than 5% of respondents departed from the majority answer (a rock, a thermostat, a monkey, other people and oneself) and keeps the other 8, with 298 complete respondents. Francken and colleagues analysed a subsample of conference attendees; this analysis uses every respondent with complete answers.

For each battery, maximum-entropy models of order one (independent answers), two and three were fitted with a quadratic penalty chosen by inner cross-validation, and their held-out log-likelihood was compared over five repetitions of ten-fold cross-validation. Each triplet of answers was also tested for third-order structure by the likelihood-ratio statistic of the model without a three-way interaction, with a null distribution simulated from the triplet's marginal under the fitted pairwise model of the whole battery. For the trolley data, with three answers and 64,988 participants, the pairwise model was fitted without a penalty by iterative scaling [@darroch1972] and compared with the saturated model by ten-fold cross-validation.

### Three trolley cases

The three answers carry 0.2050 bits of multi-information per participant. The pairwise model accounts for 0.2001 bits, and the third-order connected information is 0.00489 bits, 2.38% of the total, with a 95% bootstrap interval from 1.99% to 2.84%. In held-out data the pairwise model improves on independent answers by 0.2001 bits per participant, and the saturated model improves on the pairwise model by 0.00487 bits per participant (standard error 0.00039), in all 10 folds. The likelihood-ratio statistic for the three-way interaction is 440 on one degree of freedom.

The third-order term has a definite shape (Figure 2a). Rejecting the sacrifice in Switch and Loop while accepting it in Footbridge, the pattern 001, occurs at 0.734 times the frequency the pairwise model predicts (95% interval 0.708 to 0.759). Rejecting it in Switch while accepting it in Loop and Footbridge, the pattern 011, occurs at 1.350 times the prediction (1.317 to 1.386). In log-linear terms, the log odds ratio between the Switch and Loop answers is 1.10 larger among participants who reject the Footbridge sacrifice than among those who accept it (95% interval 1.00 to 1.21). The pairwise model captures that accepting the Footbridge sacrifice goes with accepting the other two; it underestimates how rarely people accept the Footbridge sacrifice while rejecting both others.

Mixing of countries or of design conditions does not produce the term. In each of the 13 countries with at least 1,000 participants it has the same sign, with a p-value below 0.05 in all 13 and below the Bonferroni threshold in 12. The participant-weighted mean of the within-country third-order information, 0.00464 bits, matches the 0.00462 bits of the same countries pooled. The sign is also the same within each of the 6 presentation orders of the three cases, each of the 8 left-right placements of the sacrifice option, both device types and both session types (before and after the main Moral Machine task), with weighted within-stratum means between 0.00421 and 0.00487 bits. Unobserved groups of respondents, for instance inattentive ones, could still contribute to it.

### Consciousness researchers

In the position battery the pairwise model improves held-out log-likelihood over independent answers by 0.235 bits per respondent (standard error 0.025), in 45 of 50 folds. The third-order model is worse than the pairwise model by 0.048 bits per respondent (standard error 0.008) and better in only 7 of 50 folds. None of the 165 triplets shows third-order structure after control of the false discovery rate at 5%: the smallest simulated p-value is 0.0045, and 8 triplets fall below 0.05, about the number expected by chance. The median third-order information of a triplet is 0.0011 bits, below the median of 0.0025 bits expected from the fitted pairwise model and sampling noise. The largest, 0.0196 bits, belongs to the answers on the driving case, the detection-threshold case and machine consciousness. Call a combination of three answers that never occurs, although each of its three pairs of answers does, a joint avoidance; it is the empirical trace of an obstruction of order three inside the pairwise hull. The position battery contains none, against 0.18 expected under the pairwise model.

The entity battery has much stronger pairwise structure: the pairwise model improves on independent answers by 1.675 bits per respondent (standard error 0.059), in all 50 folds. The third-order model is again worse, by 0.047 bits (standard error 0.009), better in 9 of 50 folds, and none of the 56 triplets survives control of the false discovery rate. The data contain 19 joint avoidances, fewer than the 30.0 expected under the pairwise model (95% simulation interval 14 to 50).

The survey's null result is informative only above a definite effect size. Planting third-order structure in the fitted model of the position battery while holding every one- and two-way margin fixed (Figure 2b), the triplet test reaches 80% power at a third-order information of 0.0194 bits for one pre-specified triplet at $\alpha=0.05$ (0.0184 to 0.0199 across 8 random triplets) and 0.0481 bits after Bonferroni correction over all triplets; its size at zero effect is 0.054. An effect of the size found in the trolley data is detected with probability 0.29. The survey therefore rules out strong third-order structure among the positions it asks about. It cannot detect structure of the strength present in the trolley judgments.

### A test on the PhilPapers survey

Individual responses to the 2020 PhilPapers survey would make the test decisive at the size found in the trolley data. The survey asked 100 questions [@bourget2023] and has 1,785 respondents [@shi2026]. Under the noncentral chi-square approximation, which gives 0.0193 bits for the 80% power threshold at the survey of consciousness researchers' sample size against 0.0194 bits by simulation, a pre-specified triplet answered by all 1,785 respondents would reveal an effect of the trolley size with probability 0.94 and an effect of 0.0032 bits with probability 0.8. A scan of all 161,700 triplets with Bonferroni correction would reach 80% power at 0.0144 bits. Questions answered by fewer respondents give less power.

## What a map of positions should record

A map that records distances or pairwise compatibilities between commitments decides which syntheses are available if and only if its commitment class has order two. Profiles of categorical answers and of ranges on ordered scales have that order, which is why correlation maps and distance maps of survey answers are safe for reading off compatible combinations of answers. Atlases that admit exclusions on questions with several options, conditional principles between questions, or convex regions on discrete scales do not have it, and their pairwise maps can show compatible pairs where no joint position exists. Proposition 3 turns the order of any finite atlas into a search over its positions, and Table 1 gives the order of the common classes in closed form.

What such an atlas should record follows from the order. With order two, pairwise compatibility suffices. With order $k$, the atlas has to record compatibility of $k$ commitments at a time, or the commitments themselves together with a decision procedure. Where the commitments are closed under a near-unanimity operation, issue-local propagation decides compatibility even when the commitment order is large: categorical answers joined by implications have order $n+1$, yet two issues at a time suffice to refute their obstructions. A map read for occupancy is a different object again. Whether a combination of regions is held by anyone needs checks of at least six regions at a time on any planar map of thirty positions in general position, while whether it is possible needs checks of three.

The data say how much is lost in practice when the map is statistical. In lay judgments on three trolley cases, pairwise statistics carry all but 2.38% of the multi-information, and the remainder is a stable third-order term: accepting the Footbridge sacrifice while rejecting both others is rarer than the pairwise maximum-entropy model predicts. In expert positions on consciousness, nothing beyond pairwise structure is detectable at this sample size, and an effect of the trolley size would most likely have been missed. These are two datasets, one of lay judgments and one of a specialized community, and the binary coding of five-point answers at "probably yes" is a choice that a finer model of ordinal answers could revisit.

The theory concerns crisp compatibility: a family of commitments is compatible or not. Graded commitments, credences and the probabilistic coherence of degrees of belief need a different local-to-global theory, and so do changes of the representation itself, when a new distinction splits a position the atlas treated as one. The decisive empirical test is also left open: the 2020 PhilPapers survey, analysed with the triplet test above, would show whether philosophers' positions on 100 questions contain third-order structure of the strength found in lay moral judgments, and which triplets of positions carry it.

## Code and data availability

The code, the frozen derived data and the recorded results are available at https://github.com/piatra-institute/papers-beyond-philosophical-distance. The script `simulation/fetch_data.py` downloads the two source files from the Open Science Framework, checks their SHA-256 hashes and derives the files in `simulation/data/`; `simulation/run_all.py` runs the tests, the exhaustive verification and the analyses and writes `simulation/output/results.json` and the figures. The trolley data are the file `Shared_data_allResponses.csv` of OSF project mxa6z (https://osf.io/mxa6z/), deposited by Awad and colleagues with their 2020 article; the survey data are the file `survey_jasp.csv` of OSF project xz3ua (https://osf.io/xz3ua/), deposited by Francken and colleagues with their 2022 article. Neither project states a licence; the derived files are kept for reproduction with attribution, and the raw files are not redistributed. Individual responses to the PhilPapers surveys were not available for this study.
